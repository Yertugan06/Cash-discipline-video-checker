# Senior engineer ideas — ForteBank Datathon 26

## 1. Core opportunity

**Verified from the brief and local metadata:** The submission must accept a new `.mov` through a public UI and an API-key-protected, two-step API; return a binary verdict, each event's type and time, 1–3 JPEG evidence frames, and a defensible Kazakhstan rule source; run all video analysis locally and finish each job within five minutes. Detection, localization, classification, and rule justification account for **70 of 100 points**. The label sheet has 42 rows with verdicts and integer-second timestamps, but no event types. Prior repository inspection found 40 distinct videos after two duplicate pairs; the clips are staged from one overhead camera, while a final test uses another angle. No video frames were inspected for this document.

**Engineering judgment:** The opportunity is to make *checkable event claims*, not merely classify clips. A strong entry discovers the relevant moment without being given its timestamp, shows the action across time, names only what is visible, and binds the interpretation to a verified clause. The small, untyped dataset makes shortcut learning tempting; the new angle makes fixed screen coordinates fragile. A reviewer should be able to jump from a result to the precise frames that support or refute it.

## 2. Competing solution concepts

| Concept | Mechanism and product experience | Potential edge over a conventional clip classifier | Main uncertainty | 24-hour feasibility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Temporal rule monitor** | Detect people, hands, generic objects, and role/region relations; turn their changes into events and apply short temporal predicates, including “required step absent before visible step B.” Show the triggering sequence and the linked rule. | Time, type, and explanation arise from the same observable predicates. Relational rules may transfer better than pixel coordinates. | Tiny objects, occluded hands, role confusion, and whether scene regions can be inferred automatically for the new angle. | **Medium** for a few visible mechanisms; broad coverage is unlikely in one day. |
| **B. Local video-language examiner** | Ask a commercially usable local video model to search the full clip against a compact checklist of observable actions, identify times, and describe evidence; bind outputs to a separately verified rule catalog. | Open-ended observation could catch a mechanism absent from training labels, with little per-camera setup. | Hallucinated hand/object details, missed brief actions at sparse sampling, timestamp drift, GPU memory and five-minute runtime. | **Medium** if suitable hardware and weights are ready; otherwise low. |
| **C. Few-shot visual retrieval** | Build a small human-named set of clean and violation *mechanism* examples; rank sliding video windows with frozen visual embeddings, then inspect the best matches. The UI shows similar reference sequences alongside the candidate. | Makes unusually efficient use of sparse labels and can surface recurring staged patterns without training a classifier from scratch. | It may learn actor, prop, duration, or viewpoint similarity instead of the action; unseen types have no prototype. | **High** as a search aid; **low** as the sole verdict engine. |
| **D. Coverage-first evidence cascade** | Scan the entire clip at low cost for scene changes and human/object interactions, with periodic windows even during quiet periods. Reinspect proposed windows at higher temporal density or with crops using a local video model; accept an event only when the observation and a rule condition agree. Show a short before/during/after evidence strip. | Spending compute near possible events can improve time precision and object detail while retaining full-video coverage. It combines a fast search stage with stronger verification. | The proposer might miss quiet or omission violations; the verifier might still affirm unsupported actions. Both stages need independent recall/false-alarm checks. | **Medium** with a deliberately narrow first version; integration risk is real. |

All four need the same trustworthy rule catalog. An unusual action alone is a lead for review, not proof of a violation. None of these concepts has measured performance on the supplied footage.

## 3. Promising mechanisms

- **Mechanism labels rather than scene labels:** Describe roles, object transfer, access, order, and omission without actor identity, desk side, or clip duration. This could support both prompts and temporal rules, and exposes where a rule depends on invisible facts.
- **Multi-scale time search:** Use sparse whole-clip sampling for coverage, then denser frames and person/hand crops around candidates. Preserve the original frame and exact timestamp as evidence. Compare this with whole-video inference at equal runtime; short actions could defeat either strategy.
- **Event graph with explicit provenance:** Keep “what pixels support,” “what action is inferred,” and “which clause makes it potentially improper” as separate claims. A curated clause-to-mechanism mapping prevents generated citations and makes explanation failures inspectable.
- **Negative controls and cross-checks:** Ask the verifier what evidence would contradict a proposed transfer or missing step; compare a suspected window with nearby normal activity. This is a hypothesis for reducing affirmative bias, not a proven safeguard.
- **Viewpoint-resistant cues:** Test relative relationships (person near desk, object passes between roles) before absolute screen zones. A definition that survives a camera move still needs a detector that survives it.

## 4. Strongest initial direction

**Recommendation, unvalidated:** Start with **D, the evidence cascade**, grounded in a small catalog built from both the staged examples and verified public Kazakhstan clauses. Give it two complementary routes to candidate windows: interaction/change cues for short actions, plus regular coverage and explicit procedure-state checks for quiet actions or omissions. Use a local video-language model only to examine candidate sequences and describe visible evidence; keep final rule matching in an explicit catalog. If the fast proposer loses labeled moments, favor whole-video examination (B). If the model invents decisive details, use the temporal monitor (A) for the mechanisms its primitives can truly observe.

This direction has the best *potential* balance across the 70 points tied to events and justification: broad search, precise evidence, open-ended visual interpretation, and auditable normative claims. Its main product advantage is a reviewer-ready event strip with a short, falsifiable explanation, not an opaque score. The recommendation depends on early evidence and should not be treated as an architecture decision.

## 5. Research questions

1. **What actually happens at labeled times, and can a human distinguish it at native resolution?** A blinded local viewing pass should name mechanisms, decisive frames, required temporal context, and ambiguous cases. Resolve whether the byte-identical clip labeled at both 57 and 68 seconds contains two events or noisy labels.
2. **Can a low-cost scan preserve event recall?** Run it over complete clips without using label timestamps to choose windows; count labeled moments covered at a practical candidate budget, including omissions and quiet interactions. Failure would favor B over D.
3. **Can a local model verify fine actions without affirmative bias and meet five minutes?** Compare labeled and matched clean windows, plus full-clip runtime, memory, time error, and evidence support on the available hardware. Failure would favor A or a narrower rule catalog.
4. **Which apparent mechanisms have a real, applicable public RK clause?** Verify document text and conditions against official sources; separate visual observation from any authorization, accounting, or identity fact the video cannot establish. This could remove entire categories from the reportable set.
5. **Does recognition survive the changed viewpoint, and how will the jury read rule citations?** Test one independently filmed angle with the same mechanisms; ask whether API clients may receive a rule/source field, since the required UI asks for citations but the sample event JSON omits one. Evidence here determines whether D's relational cues and its output contract are viable.
